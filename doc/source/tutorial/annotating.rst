Annotating course materials
---------------------------


In order for `automata` to automatically generate a course webpage from your
materials, you must tell it where the materials are, when they should be
released, and how to build them (if a build step is necessary). This is done by
*annotating* each of your course materials. Once this is done, `automata` will
be able to discover them by searching the filesystem.

Artifacts, publications, and collections
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Before getting into the nuts and bolts of how, exactly, course materials are
annotated, it's useful to first talk about how `automata` organizes them. Two
different courses can have very different course materials, but they are likely
to fit into the hierarchy described below.

At the bottom of `automata`'s hierarchy is an **artifact**. In `automata`, an
artifact is a file, usually one meant for distribution to students as part
of the course materials. For example, artifacts in a typical course might
include ``homework.pdf`` (a PDF of the first homework's questions),
``solution.pdf`` (a PDF of the solutions), and ``template.zip`` (a starter
template students can use for their write-up). An artifact might be the result
of some build process, such as the case with a PDF generated by LaTeX, but it
can also be a simple unchanging file. A typical course might have dozens or
even hundreds of artifacts.

One level up, artifacts are naturally organized into **publications**.
Abstractly, a publication is a coherent grouping of one or more artifacts and
their metadata. More concretely, consider again the artifacts above
(``homework.pdf``, ``solution.pdf``, and ``template.zip``). All three artifacts
are associated with the first homework, and so the publication containing them
is simply "Homework 01". Some metadata we might want to associate with this
publication might include the homework's due date and the topic. A typical
course might have dozens of publications, each with several artifacts.

Next, `automata` organizes publications into **collections**. A typical course might
have a "homeworks" collection, a "lectures" collection, a "quizzes" collection, and so
on.

We give the hierarchy described above the name **"course materials
hierarchy"**. The hierarchy can be viewed as a tree. The leaf nodes of the tree
are artifacts (files). One level up are publications, and a level higher are
collections. In `automata`, the root of the tree is called the "universe",
because it contains everything.
